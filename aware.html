QBurst · Oldcastle Materials

Keeping highway work-zone crews out of harm’s way

AWAREConstruction safetyiOS, Android & WebUX/UI

Brief

Every day, highway and street construction workers are at risk of fatal and serious injuries while working near passing motorists, construction vehicles and other equipment. Each year, more than 100 workers are killed and over 20,000 injured in the highway and street construction industry.

Oldcastle Materials is the holding company for CRH's American operations and a leading supplier of aggregates, asphalt and construction services in the US.

From the fall of 2012 to the spring of 2013, Oldcastle Materials lost four of their members nationwide. Three of those four employees were lost within an 18-hour period. As with all such fatalities, it was devastating, and some workers left their jobs for a safer working environment.

Oldcastle Materials decided to invest in the lives of their employees with a system that would combine human effort with cutting-edge technology: a system that would always be vigilant, working and non-fatigued.

Oldcastle partnered with ARTIS, LLC to develop an innovative work zone intrusion detection and alarm system called Advance Warning And Risk Evasion (AWARE). QBurst was approached by Oldcastle to transform the existing proof-of-concept application into a user-friendly application with expanded features and functionality, to keep their workers safe.

The system

AWARE is a radar-based system that can detect a potential work zone intrusion from multiple vehicles and, simultaneously, warn the errant driver and the workers who may be in harm's way. It detects a threat by determining speed, position, trajectory and stopping distance, providing valuable seconds that can save the lives of workers and the motoring public.

One sensor includes electronically-scanned radar, high-precision differential GPS, accelerometers, gyroscopes and magnetometers for position and orientation sensing, plus high-definition video and several wireless interfaces to monitor traffic and broadcast a warning when an intrusion is detected.

A second sensor is a tracking device strapped to the workers' hard hats, vests or armbands. If a worker is in the path of an oncoming threat, a vibration motor and acoustic buzzer alert them, and lights and audible alerts are activated.

Challenge

First, I had to understand how the whole worksite functions, from the beginning of a worker's day, through existing safety procedures, to how they interact with the sensors. Second, I had to examine how the radar system works and how the sensors communicate with each other, and consider the technical feasibility of each interaction and function on the interface.

The hardest part was working within a shoestring budget and the time allotted to the UX phase: less than two weeks.

“A small error or delay in the feedback can lead to major casualties!”

My role

I led the QBurst design team for the AWARE app across iOS, Android and web from the start of the project in February 2017, identifying and addressing customer pain points and refining the design based on user testing and technical challenges. It was also my responsibility to make sure the initial UX phase was completed on time.

I followed a human-centred, lean UX design process, so that my design decisions were supported by user research and feedback.

Discover

User research

I needed to understand how a road construction site works, and the needs, motivations and working style of the people the app was for. What could be better than a direct visit? I spent two weeks on site in Utah, USA.

We visited a construction site with the project coordinators from Oldcastle Materials and ARTIS and interviewed users to learn their concerns, needs and mental models. We even built a miniature work site in our conference room to refer to during discussions.

Competitive analysis

I looked at other construction safety systems in use. None of them had a mobile application to control the entire system, and most of the sensors were basic.

Key insights

  • The primary users of the mobile app are not used to advanced technology.
  • Most primary users are aged 25 to 45.
  • Users might work in extreme conditions: smoke, dust and heat.
  • Site supervisors need to switch the radars on and off manually several times during working hours.
  • Work sites might be in remote locations without good connectivity.
  • Administrators cannot assess how effective the system is from offsite.
  • The radars were not yet fully functional, so several technical challenges had to be anticipated in the UI.
  • Construction workers wear gloves and goggles, so they need large touch targets.

Define

Personas

From the research and validated assumptions, I created personas to capture the traits of the potential users and check whether features would be usable.

Application map

During brainstorming sessions with the Oldcastle and ARTIS teams, I created a mind map of the entire system. It helped us consider every feature discussed and bridged gaps in later conversations about user needs and the features stakeholders wanted.

User flows

I created user flows for the main tasks, which helped reduce the number of steps a user needs to complete each task.

Key insights

  • A single tap to switch the radars on site on or off was handy.
  • A visualiser screen to watch videos of intrusions was practical.
  • A map of the location showing the position of assets on the work site was useful.
  • Information on how to align a Raven was hard to find and understand.
  • Seeing the alerts generated by different sensors on site was slightly confusing for a few users.

Design

Paper sketches

Based on the research and key task flows, I sketched the key screens on the spot so I could get feedback from the whole team, and iterated until we reached common ground.

Wireframes

The sketches then became higher-fidelity, clickable wireframes in Axure, used to gather early feedback from users and stakeholders.

Prototype

After that feedback, the wireframes were refined and moved into branding and visual design. The screens were linked into an InVision prototype for user testing, some of it in person, which gave me a better understanding of users' thinking.

User testing

I ran user testing with 9 users, who were asked to complete a set of tasks. I found patterns where users were confused and made errors, including confusing wording in some places, and revised the wireframes based on the results.

Conclusion

What would I do next?

We received a lot of positive feedback from the users we tested, along with a few negative points. Unfortunately, we didn't have much time for the UX research phase. With more time and budget, I would test on an actual work site, which would lead to further iterations and a more intuitive interface.
